Grease rules for Costa Mesa food businesses

The Costa Mesa Sanitary District requires every food service establishment to hold a wastewater discharge permit under its fats, oils and grease control program. The goal is to keep grease out of the public sewer, where it causes blockages and spills.

For a restaurant, that means keeping the grease interceptor serviced and the lines between the kitchen and the interceptor clear. Interceptor sizing and installation fall under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). Scheduled high-pressure jetting keeps those lines from closing up during a Friday dinner rush.

A sewer backup in a food business can force a closure until it is cleaned up. Scheduled line maintenance costs far less than one lost service.

Annual backflow testing

Mesa Water District requires backflow prevention assemblies to be tested every year, under the federal Safe Drinking Water Act amendments, the California Code of Regulations and the district's own rules. Most commercial properties have at least one assembly, on the domestic supply, irrigation or fire line.

Our backflow testing service covers the annual test, repairs when an assembly fails, and the paperwork the utility needs.

Hot water rules in food businesses

California's Retail Food Code requires handwashing sinks in food facilities to deliver warm water, at least 100 degrees Fahrenheit. When a water heater fails in a restaurant, an inspector can treat it as a reason to stop service until hot water is back.

That makes sizing matter. A commercial kitchen often runs a dish machine, prep sinks and handwashing sinks at once, and a residential-size tank can fall behind during a rush. We size replacements to the real peak demand, whether that means a larger tank, several tankless units, or a booster. For single units, see sizing a new water heater.

Restrooms that see heavy use

Offices, gyms and retail restrooms wear out flush valves, wax seals and faucet cartridges far faster than a home bathroom. A flush valve that sticks open can run for hours on a weekend before staff notice it on Monday.

The water here is disinfected with chloramines, which can shorten the life of some rubber seals. Choosing chloramine-resistant replacement parts is a small detail that cuts repeat calls.

Commercial leak detection

Hidden leaks in commercial buildings are expensive because they run unnoticed. A ceiling stain in an office suite, a water bill that jumps for a retail center, or water under a restaurant floor mat can each trace back to a supply line inside a wall or under the slab.

We isolate the building by zone, test after hours so water can be shut off safely, and use acoustic and thermal tools to pinpoint the leak before anything is opened. The owner, manager and tenant receive the same written findings, which settles who is responsible.

The full process is on our commercial leak testing page, and the residential version is described on our leak detection page.

Tenant improvements and new fixtures

When a space changes hands, the plumbing often has to change with it. A new salon needs more shampoo sinks, a cafe needs a three-compartment sink and a hand sink, and an office conversion may need a new restroom.

That work needs permits and inspection, and it has to fit the building's existing drain and water capacity. We check the capacity first, then rough in the new fixtures, coordinate with the other trades, and set the fixtures once the finishes are in. Food businesses also need their layout to satisfy the sanitary district's grease control rules.

Planning plumbing early in a tenant improvement avoids the most expensive surprise: finding out after the floor is poured that the drain cannot reach where the sink was drawn.

Multi-unit buildings

Apartment and condo buildings share drain stacks, water heaters and supply risers, so one failure can affect many residents. A clogged stack backs up into the lowest units first. A failed riser can take water away from a whole floor.

We schedule shutdowns with notice, keep outages short, and give the owner a written record of what was found and fixed. For buildings with aging supply lines, replacing the supply lines can be staged floor by floor while residents stay in place.
