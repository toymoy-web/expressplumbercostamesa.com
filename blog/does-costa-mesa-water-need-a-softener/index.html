Does Costa Mesa Water Need a Softener? What Mesa Water's Numbers Say

Written by the Costa Mesa Express Plumbing team. Published September 24, 2026 · Updated September 24, 2026.

Costa Mesa water is moderately hard on average, not very hard. Mesa Water's 2025 Water Quality Report lists 2024 groundwater hardness at an average of 109 parts per million, about 6.4 grains per gallon, with a range from roughly 1.2 to 14 grains depending on the source mix.

Many homes do fine without a softener. It makes the most sense for homes that test toward the hard end, households with a tankless water heater, and anyone bothered by spotting and scale.

Here is how to read those numbers, how to test your own tap, and how to decide. Homes in Fountain Valley get water from a different utility, so these figures apply to Mesa Water customers.

The numbers, in context

Hardness is reported in parts per million (ppm) or grains per gallon (gpg). One grain per gallon is about 17.1 ppm.

Common water-industry scales put about 3.5 to 7 gpg in the moderately hard range and above about 10.5 gpg in the very hard range. Costa Mesa's average sits at the upper end of moderate.

Why the range is so wide

Mesa Water blends water from different wells. Most comes from shallow wells in the Orange County Groundwater Basin, and a share comes from deep amber-water wells at the Mesa Water Reliability Facility, which the district describes as naturally soft. The blend reaching your street can differ from your neighbor's a mile away, and it can shift over time.

That is why the average alone does not tell you what is in your glass. A reading at your own tap is the only number worth basing a purchase on.

Some Costa Mesa addresses are served by IRWD instead, with a different supply and different figures. Your water bill tells you which utility you have.

Test your own water

  1. Use cold water from the kitchen. Run it for a minute before sampling.
  2. Use a hardness test strip or kit. Many hardware stores sell inexpensive ones.
  3. Read in grains per gallon. Convert from ppm by dividing by about 17.1 if needed.
  4. Test twice, weeks apart. The blend can change, so two readings are better than one.

A plumber can test at the visit too. Ask for the reading in writing with any softener quote.

Salt-based or salt-free

A salt-based softener swaps hardness minerals for sodium and gives the familiar soft-water feel. It needs salt refills and discharges brine to the sewer when it regenerates. California law allows local agencies to restrict salt-discharging softeners, so check current rules with your sewer agency before installing one.

Salt-free conditioners change how minerals behave so they scale less, without removing them. Water does not feel soft, but equipment sees less buildup and there is no brine discharge.

Whichever you choose, the regeneration drain must discharge through an air gap under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

What a softener will not fix

Softening does not remove chloramine, the disinfectant Mesa Water uses. If the water tastes or smells of disinfectant, water filtration with catalytic carbon is the right tool. Softening also does not fix rusty water from old pipes, which is a plumbing problem, not a water chemistry one.

It will not rescue a water heater that is already failing. If your tank is rumbling with sediment or leaking, see our water heater repair page first.

Sizing a softener for your household

If you do decide on a softener, size it from your own hardness reading and your household's water use, not from a generic chart. A softener's capacity is rated in grains, and it regenerates after removing that many grains of hardness. A system that is too small regenerates constantly, wasting salt and water. One that is far too large can let water sit in the resin tank between regenerations.

The math is simple. Multiply your hardness in grains per gallon by the gallons your household uses in a day. That is the daily grain load, and a good installer uses it to set both the system size and the regeneration schedule. At Costa Mesa's moderate average, many households need a smaller unit than the ones sold for harder inland water.

Cheaper ways to manage moderate hardness

At a moderate level, simple maintenance goes a long way. Flush a tank water heater once a year. Descale a tankless unit every one to two years. Soak showerheads and faucet aerators in white vinegar when flow drops. Wipe glass shower doors after use.

If after a year of that you still want softer water, a system is a reasonable upgrade. Our softener installation page starts every quote with a test at your tap.
