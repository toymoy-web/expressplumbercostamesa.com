Slab Leaks in Postwar Costa Mesa Ranch Homes: What to Watch For and What to Do

Written by the Costa Mesa Express Plumbing team. Published September 24, 2026 · Updated October 12, 2026.

A slab leak is a leak in a water line that runs in or under a home's concrete floor. In Costa Mesa, they show up most in single-story ranch homes from the 1950s and 1960s, where supply lines were laid under the slab and have now been in service for 60 years or more.

The early signs are indirect: a warm spot on the floor, the sound of water running when nothing is on, a meter that keeps turning, or a water bill that jumps.

This guide explains why these homes get slab leaks, how to confirm one, and how to choose the right fix.

Why these homes, and why now

Costa Mesa's building boom came right after the city incorporated in 1953. Many homes from that era are single-story ranch houses on concrete slabs, with copper or galvanized supply lines laid beneath the floor before the concrete was poured.

Decades later, those lines wear in predictable ways. Pipe that touches concrete or gravel can abrade as it expands and contracts. Hot water lines, which expand the most, tend to fail first. Once one pinhole appears, it is common for others to follow on the same line, because the whole run is the same age.

Early signs worth taking seriously

None of these alone proves a slab leak. Two or more together are worth a test.

  • A warm or hot patch on tile or concrete, often near the kitchen, a bathroom or the water heater.
  • The sound of running or hissing water under the floor with every fixture off.
  • The water meter's leak indicator moving when nothing in the house is using water.
  • A water heater that seems to run more often than it used to.
  • Damp carpet, cupping wood floors or cracked grout with no fixture nearby.
  • A jump in the water bill that household habits do not explain.

How a slab leak is confirmed and located

  1. Meter test. With everything off, a moving leak indicator confirms water is escaping.
  2. Isolation. Closing valves separates the hot side from the cold side and the service line from the house.
  3. Thermal imaging. A hot line leak shows as a warm trail across the slab.
  4. Acoustic listening. Floor microphones pick up the sound of water escaping under pressure.
  5. Mark the spot. The leak is narrowed to a small area before anything is opened.

The detection process is covered in detail on our leak detection page.

Repair, reroute or repipe: how to choose

Once the leak is marked, three honest options remain. The pipe's condition matters more than the single leak.

What insurance usually does and does not pay

Homeowner policies vary, so read yours. In general, many policies pay for water damage from a sudden leak, and some pay to access the pipe by breaking and restoring the floor. The failed pipe itself is usually treated as wear and not covered.

Some insurers will pay toward a reroute in place of breaking the slab, since it avoids tear-out costs. Ask before you choose a method. Keep the leak detection report and photos of any damage, since adjusters commonly ask for both.

A slow slab leak that has run for months may be treated differently from a sudden one. Acting quickly on the first signs helps both the repair and the claim.

Living with it until the repair

A slab leak is rarely a flood, but it should not wait long. Water under a slab can soften the soil, damage flooring and, in a hot line, waste energy heating water that disappears into the ground.

If the leak is on the hot side, closing the cold inlet valve on the water heater stops the loss while leaving cold water on in the house. If it is on the cold side, you may need to close the main shutoff between uses. A plumber can often isolate the leaking branch so most of the house keeps water.

Neighbors, same era, same problem

Because whole streets in Costa Mesa, Fountain Valley and Westminster went up in the same years, homes on the same block often share the same plumbing history. If a neighbor has just repiped after slab leaks, it is worth checking your own meter. For homes in those nearby cities, see our Fountain Valley page for local notes.

Hot or cold side: why it matters

Knowing which side is leaking changes the plan. A hot-side leak wastes both water and the energy used to heat it, and it can be stopped overnight by closing the water heater's cold inlet. A cold-side leak runs at full street pressure all the time, so it usually needs the main shutoff closed between uses.

After the fix

Whatever the method, the new pipe should be pressure-tested before the floor or wall is closed, the same acceptance test the California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), uses for new piping. Keep the permit and invoice with your home records. A documented reroute or repipe answers a buyer's questions later.

It is also worth checking incoming water pressure. Pressure above 80 psi stresses every line in the house, and the code calls for a reducing valve at that level.

Costa Mesa note: finding the leak is the owner's job

Mesa Water's rules say the district does not investigate leaks on private property, so a leak under your slab is the owner's to find and fix. In an emergency you may close the Customer Control Valve at the meter, the only valve the district's rules let customers operate.

Homes served by IRWD, a small part of the city, should ask about its leak adjustment, which re-bills usage from the higher tiers at the base rate after the repair. The same postwar slab construction continues into Westminster, where these leaks follow the same pattern.
