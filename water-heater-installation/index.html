Water Heater Installation in Costa Mesa, CA

Water heater installation means swapping out a failed tank for a new unit. The new one gets sized, vented, strapped and permitted for your home. Signs your water heater needs replacing, not repair

A repair makes sense on a newer tank with one failed part. These signs point the other way.

  • Water pooling under the tank or rust streaks running down the jacket. A leaking tank body cannot be patched.
  • The tank is past 10 to 12 years old and has needed more than one repair in the last two years.
  • Rusty or brown water only from the hot side, which usually means the tank lining is failing.
  • Popping or rumbling while it heats, a sign of heavy sediment on the bottom of the tank.
  • Hot water that runs out far faster than it used to, even after a flush.

If the tank is younger and the problem is a thermocouple, gas valve or element, a heater repair is the cheaper, honest answer.

What California code adds to a Costa Mesa water heater swap

California law requires every water heater to be braced against earthquakes. The plumbing code sets it at two straps, one in the upper third of the tank and one in the lower third. Many older Costa Mesa garages still have a single strap or plumber's tape, so we replace that on every install.

A gas unit in a garage has to sit at least 18 inches off the floor unless it is a model built to resist flammable vapor ignition. The temperature and pressure relief line must run to a safe spot outside. If your home has a pressure reducing valve or a check valve on the main line, the plumbing is a closed system and needs an expansion tank.

Each of these items is checked by the City of Costa Mesa inspector when the permit is closed. Skipping them can come back to bite you at resale, when a buyer's inspector flags an unpermitted heater.

Seismic bracing is not optional in California. Health and Safety Code section 19211 makes it a condition of every new or replacement installation.

Can you still replace a gas water heater with gas in Costa Mesa?

Yes, as of September 2026. On June 6, 2025, the South Coast Air Quality Management District board voted 7 to 5 against proposed changes to Rule 1121 that would have pushed new water heater sales toward zero emission units. The existing rule stayed in place, so a gas-to-gas swap in an existing home is still allowed.

Gas water heaters sold in the district still have to meet the current low-NOx limits, which every compliant model on the market already does. If you are thinking about a heat pump anyway, ask about current Southern California Edison and state incentive programs before you buy, since amounts change often.

How a replacement goes, start to finish

Most straight tank swaps take one visit of about three to five hours.

  1. Shutoff and drain. We close the gas and water, drain the old tank and disconnect it.
  2. Remove and set. The old unit is hauled out and the new one is set, leveled and raised where code requires.
  3. Connect and upgrade. New flex connectors, straps, relief line, drip pan where needed and an expansion tank on closed systems.
  4. Light and test. We fill, purge air, light or power the unit and check for gas and water leaks.
  5. Permit. The permit is filed with the city and the inspection is scheduled with you.

How Costa Mesa water affects tank life

Mesa Water's 2025 report puts its 2024 groundwater hardness at an average of 6.4 grains per gallon, with a range from 1.2 to 14. That is moderate, so scale is slower here than in inland cities. Sediment still settles, and a yearly flush through the drain valve is the cheapest way to add years to a tank.

A few Costa Mesa addresses get their water from IRWD instead, with a different mix. If you are not sure which utility you have, the name on your water bill tells you.

A water heater that sits in a finished closet or attic should have a drip pan piped to a drain. When it leaks, the pan decides whether you clean a garage floor or replace a ceiling.
