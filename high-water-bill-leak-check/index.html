High Water Bill Leak Check in Costa Mesa, CA

A high water bill leak check tracks down why your bill jumped when your habits did not change. In Costa Mesa, CA, the answer is usually one of a handful of things, and a meter test at the start separates a real leak from a billing or usage change in minutes.

The bill doubled and nothing looks wet

These are the patterns that bring most people to us. Each one points somewhere different.

  • The bill rose sharply in one cycle, with the same people and routines at home.
  • The bill has crept up month after month.
  • The meter's small indicator moves when every tap and appliance is off.
  • You hear a faint hiss or trickle at night.
  • A strip of lawn or a planter bed stays wet between waterings.

The usual suspects, ranked

Most high bills trace back to one of these. The first three are the most common and the cheapest to fix.

What happens on a high-bill visit

  1. Meter test. Everything off, then we watch the indicator.
  2. Split the system. Closing the house valve separates the buried line from indoor plumbing.
  3. Check the easy ones. Every toilet, the softener, the irrigation valves and the pool fill.
  4. Go hidden if needed. Acoustic and thermal tools for slab, wall and yard lines.
  5. Write it down. What was found, where, and what was fixed.

The detection tools themselves are described on our hidden leak detection page.

Talking to your water utility

Most Costa Mesa homes buy water from Mesa Water District, and some from the Irvine Ranch Water District. The meter and the street side belong to them. Everything on your side of the meter is generally the owner's.

Some utilities review a high bill after a leak on the customer side has been repaired. Ask yours what it needs. A plumber's note naming the leak and the repair date is often part of it, and we provide one.

If the meter itself looks damaged or leaks inside the box, that is the utility's equipment. Call them first.

Take a photo of the meter reading before and after any repair. It is simple proof the leak stopped.

When the cause is the house itself

In homes built in the 1950s and 1960s, a hidden leak under the slab or in an exterior wall is a real possibility, especially on a hot water line. A slab leak can run for weeks before it shows anywhere but the bill.

If the test points below the floor, the slab leak page compares a spot fix with rerouting the line. Repairs are inspected under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

Irrigation is a frequent culprit in neighborhoods with big lawns, such as Mesa Verde near the golf courses and College Park near Orange Coast College, where a stuck valve can run all night without anyone noticing.

Irrigation and sprinkler line leaks

Irrigation is one of the most common places a high bill hides, because the leak only runs when the controller does. A cracked line or a valve that will not close fully can waste water for weeks before a soggy patch shows up. Look for a strip of grass or weeds that stays greener than the rest of the yard.

We test the irrigation side separately from the house. With the controller off and the irrigation shutoff closed, the meter shows whether the house or the yard is losing water. Then we run each zone and watch for a valve that keeps flowing after it should stop.

Mesa Water's conservation rules prohibit runoff and require leaks to be fixed within four days of discovery or of a notice from the district. If a notice arrived, keep it with the repair invoice.
